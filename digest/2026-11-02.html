I Don't Fear AI Slop. I Fear Untold Stories - Zack Proser, WorkOS

AI Engineer · Zack Proser, WorkOS · uploaded 2 Nov, 5:30 AM IST · 18:34
Verdict: skim from 3:47 — a short, upbeat talk; the Blog Bot details are the useful part, the rest is a personal essay.
🔁 Repeat: 'software factory' — seen 15 times before. New here: the "factory" is a personal one, a codified version of one expert's taste handed to colleagues (Blog Bot) or to his own kids (a bedtime-story app).
🔁 Repeat: 'AI fluency / enablement' — seen 8 times before. New here: the concrete Blog Bot setup and the "is it true, useful, beautiful" bar for AI-assisted writing.
Untold stories, not slop
  • The real worry: Proser has written obsessively for 15 years (one of his best-performing posts took 23 minutes, pre-LLM) and says the bigger loss isn't AI slop but colleagues who never publish because they think "I'm not a writer" (0:47–3:53)
  • Blog Bot at WorkOS: A Slack bot on Cloudflare that pulls Granola recordings, event photos, Slack threads and brand framing, then drafts an on-brand post that is 85–90% done; it can ask a human to confirm facts and has an image endpoint tuned to look like the design team's work (3:53–6:57)
  • Asymmetric leverage: A very simple interface hides a complex distributed system, and colleagues can tune drafts themselves without involving him; publishing rates rose across the org (4:38–6:57) (9:13–9:58)
Keeping craft at speed
  • Craft runs through the line: WorkOS founder Michael Grinich compared it to a cymbal factory where machines do the heavy lifting but a veteran musician plays and signs off on every unit (6:57–9:13)
  • Spark, bellows, quench: Proser decides what's worth telling, steers 14 or so LLM iterations over 2.5–3 hours (about 6x faster than before), then hand-edits and puts his name on it; the test is "is it true, is it useful, is it beautiful, does it help anyone" (9:58–13:01)
A factory for two
  • Bedtime-story app: Built with Claude Opus in 4 hours, it turns photos of his toddlers' day into a narrated, animated story starring them, with real-time voice so "again, again" replays it; kids ask for about three repeats per story (13:46–17:34)
  • The ask: Package whatever you're expert at into a tool and hand it to someone who doesn't normally make that kind of thing (17:34–18:21)

I Made a Fruit Fly Review My Code - Nick Nisi, WorkOS

AI Engineer · Nick Nisi, WorkOS · uploaded 2 Nov, 1:30 AM IST · 16:37
Verdict: skim from 3:49 — the fly demo is fun but not practical; the escalation pipeline at 12:09 is the takeaway.
🔁 Repeat: 'AI-generated code validation' — seen 30 times before. New here: a simulated fruit-fly brain as a novelty code reviewer, and a concrete escalate-only-what-matters review pipeline.
🔁 Repeat: 'human-in-the-loop review' — seen 27 times before. New here: per-team review skills tied to GitHub code owners decide when a human is pulled in.
Humans are the bottleneck
  • Review is the queue (repeat): Code and PRs are coming faster than ever, CI and review bots like Graphite and CodeRabbit already check them, and the human reviewer is what everything waits on; Nisi argues review should shift to bringing context and the "why" back to humans (0:46–2:35)
The fruit-fly reviewer
  • The brain: He used the scanned male fruit-fly connectome (about 176,000 neurons, 6.3 million connections, 125 million synapses) unchanged except for a small set of neurons used for memory (Better Stack covered this brain in September) (3:49–4:34)
  • Code as smells: A small model asks 30 neutral questions about each diff and feeds the answers in as smells; training on the codebase gives good code a sugar taste and buggy code a bitter one via dopamine signals, and the fly's tongue coming out means "approve" (4:34–7:34)
  • Does the fly matter?: Removing its memories makes it behave randomly, so the brain is doing real work; it scored about 88% in his tests (96% and 98% on two harder cases), and the room beat it 3-2 in a live game; you can try it at fruitfly.review (7:34–12:09)
Escalate only what needs a human
  • Review pipeline: A team-maintained review skill (matched via GitHub code owners), then a lightweight classifier for complexity and risk, then an automated reviewer; any failure escalates to a human, otherwise the PR can merge on its own, and misses get fed back into the skill (12:09–14:26)
  • Proof instead of review: Agents should attach screenshots, videos, test output and runnable smoke tests so humans verify behaviour rather than read code (14:26–15:57)

Why We Rebuilt Microsoft Office From Scratch for AI Agents - Yijia Liang, Compound AI

AI Engineer · Yijia Liang, Compound AI · uploaded 1 Nov, 3:30 PM IST · 15:45
Verdict: worth watching from 5:21 — the agent-rebuilt Office numbers are genuinely new; the finance framing is familiar.
🔁 Repeat: 'AI for finance' — seen 19 times before. New here: an agent-built, headless clone of Excel, PowerPoint and Word, and email inboxes for every agent.
🔁 Repeat: 'agentic search' — seen 5 times before. New here: little; grep-based agent swarms over 15,000-file data rooms instead of a vector DB is the familiar pattern.
Why finance needs different agents
  • What made personal agents work: Reliable background tasks, proactivity and integrations; in buy-side finance the equivalent is turning a data room into IC models, slides and memos (normally ~4 weeks) in days, with no rogue proactivity and context that can be 15,000 files or petabytes in SharePoint (0:47–3:04)
  • Multiplayer through email: Every Compound agent gets its own inbox so it can sit in 20-message Outlook threads; that needs per-thread auth under NDA and deliberate latency so it doesn't spam acknowledgements (3:04–5:21)
Rebuilding Office
  • Agent-written Office: Desktop plugins, VMs running Office and engines like openpyxl all fell short, so they looped agents with real Office as the judge, spending 1.5 trillion tokens and over 8 million lines of code; Excel started from the open-source IronCalc and became about 800,000 lines of Rust, with pixel-perfect Rust renderers (5:21–7:38)
  • Why headless wins: No single-machine CPU limit, parallel tasks, and 20 MB workbooks recalculate about 4x faster; owning the editor gives comment-driven editing (her slide deck was made that way) and a write-render-check-fix loop that openpyxl can't do (7:38–9:55)
Handling huge data
  • Breadth and depth: A lead agent splits the data room and swarms read files with grep, with every finding cited; for a 30 MB, 2-million-formula model, their engine pre-digests the workbook so the agent reads it faster and cheaper than code-printing approaches (9:55–12:11)
  • Copilot to autopilot: She compares finance now to coding after Opus 4.5, when people started delegating whole tasks such as a 50-page IC deck (12:58–15:16)

Most of Your Docs Readers Are Now AI Agents - Hahnbee Lee, Mintlify & Lincoln Murr, Coinbase

AI Engineer · Hahnbee Lee (Mintlify) & Lincoln Murr (Coinbase) · uploaded 1 Nov, 1:30 PM IST · 18:52
Verdict: skim from 1:34 — the docs-for-agents stats are new; the Coinbase half largely repeats Murr's 20 Oct talk.
🔁 Repeat: 'agent payments / x402' — seen 6 times before. Lincoln Murr gave a very similar x402 talk on 20 Oct ("Why AI Agents Will Need Their Own Bank Accounts"). New here: the "100 million agentic transactions in the past year" figure and the DAO angle.
🔁 Repeat: 'agentic commerce' — seen 4 times before. New here: Mintlify's numbers on agents reading docs.
Docs when agents are the reader
  • What matters now: Agents don't see your design, so page size (context use) and accuracy matter most; if an agent hits friction while following your docs it can just as easily churn to another product (1:34–3:06)
  • Agent traffic 10% to 80%: Across Mintlify-hosted docs, agents went from about 10% of viewers at the start of the year to about 80%, faster than their internal bets; human traffic hasn't fallen, agent traffic just exploded (12:09–13:40)
  • What to automate: Lee says creative prose still needs a human, but the tedious part of technical writing (finding related PRs, Slack decisions, where in the docs to inject it) can be automated (13:40–16:46)
Agents that pay (repeat)
  • APIs first: Coinbase is exposing everything humans can do through APIs, with guardrails, as a hedge against being disintermediated; APIs are the base layer, MCP and CLI are wrappers on top (3:06–5:23) (8:24–9:54)
  • x402 and micropayments (repeat): x402 now sits in a foundation co-run by Cloudflare, Stripe, Amex, Mastercard and Visa; Murr cites about 100 million on-chain agent transactions in the past year, mostly agents paying per call for APIs instead of signing up and getting keys (5:23–8:24)
  • Where it goes: Agents with money could buy paywalled research overnight or hire a lawyer to form an LLC, finally delivering the original DAO idea of automation at the core and humans at the edges (9:09–11:24)
Pitfall
  • Build vs buy: Lee's biggest pitfall is teams building docs tooling in a hackathon that nobody wants to maintain (16:46–18:18)

If Your AI Scores 100%, Your Eval Is Wrong - Ramakrishnan Lokanathan, eslams.com

AI Engineer · Ramakrishnan Lokanathan, eslams.com · uploaded 1 Nov, 12:30 PM IST · 18:45
Verdict: skip, or skim 9:17–12:19 for the games-as-evals idea; the rest is well-covered eval advice.
🔁 Repeat: 'evals for agents' — seen 51 times before. New here: only the games-as-evals idea (the open-source eSlams package); the five principles are standard advice.
Framing
  • Evals are exams (repeat): An eval is a bounded test like the SAT, one signal rather than the full picture, so don't over-index on one benchmark; the real question is whether performance matches what you promised users (0:12–3:12)
  • Why now: He thinks evals matter less in a decade, but cites the MIT "GenAI divide" report (95% of organisations seeing no return) as the cost of skipping them today (4:43–5:28)
Five principles
  • Aim higher than 100% (repeat): Define the most ambitious success first (what must happen, what must never happen, when to stop); if your eval scores 100 or even 85–90, make it harder (5:28–6:59) (17:39–18:26)
  • Test every step (repeat): An image edit asked only to change the background came back with two handles on the mug; test each step of a workflow with a test designed for that step, such as tool-call accuracy (6:14–7:45) (15:22–16:54)
  • One score isn't the system (repeat): Run evals in CI on every deploy, keep humans reviewing results, and add adversarial tests (7:45–9:17)
  • Regressions and real failures (repeat): Google Assistant's move to Gemini broke reminders, an example of not testing what used to work; route thumbs-down feedback into reproducible eval cases (12:19–14:37)
Games as evals
  • eSlams: An open-source Python package that makes models play about 50 games (chess and Go for planning, poker for hidden information, cooperation and arcade games) across 90 providers, for example pitting your old agent against the new one; a "Grand Slams of AI" tournament is planned for February (9:17–12:19)

This Little Bar Is Eating Your Claude Limits

Better Stack · uploaded 2 Nov, 12:45 AM IST · 5:27
Verdict: worth watching if you use Claude Code background agents — 5 minutes, concrete numbers and a fix.
🔁 Repeat: 'token costs / AI spend' — seen 16 times before. New here: a specific hidden cost, Claude Code's background-agent summary bar, measured with OpenTelemetry.
What the bar costs
  • The mechanism: Every 30 seconds Claude Code sends each working background agent's entire conversation to the model and asks for a 3–5 word status; this was spotted by Flamerix and the presenter verified it (0:00–0:45)
  • Measured cost: With Claude Code's OpenTelemetry export sent to Better Stack, eight 20-second sleeps produced "agent summary" calls every 30 seconds, each about 40,000 input tokens for about 130 output tokens, as context grew from 18 to 42 messages; that was 12% of the session (0:45–1:30)
  • Realistic run: Building a TanStack site with three background agents, summaries were about 8% of usage, all on Opus rather than Haiku; Flamerix's team saw 15–20%, an Anthropic engineer said about 2% overall (6% in the terminal), and the calls don't appear in the JSONL logs that tools like ccusage read (1:30–3:01)
Turning it off
  • Context: It hit a nerve because users report limits draining faster; on 28 Oct the Claude Code lead said there was no single root cause, and a GitHub issue from August measured it at almost a quarter of requests (3:01–3:46)
  • Workarounds: There's no setting, but turning off forked sub-agents (which inherit your whole conversation) takes summaries to zero while normal background sub-agents still work; Flamerix's Bun preload script blocks the timer, but pin and read it before installing (3:46–5:20)

This Code Is Full of ******

Better Stack · uploaded 1 Nov, 3:30 PM IST · 6:34
Verdict: worth watching — short, with fresh numbers on refusal tricks.
🔁 Repeat: 'agent security' — seen 28 times before, and 'prompt injection' 7 times. New here: hiding scary text in code on purpose so AI models refuse to analyse it, used by both attackers and defenders.
The script AIs won't read
  • The find: A researcher crawling the top 10,000 sites found a fingerprinting script with poison-themed variable names and invisible characters that decode to a fake AI instruction stuffed with safety-filter triggers, even Tiananmen Square to stop Chinese models (0:00–1:32)
  • Test results: Asked to deobfuscate it, GPT-6 Astra was blocked for biological risk, and Claude Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.1 were cut off by filters after a few thousand characters; DeepSeek, GLM and Kimi just did it, with GLM spotting and ignoring the messages aimed at it; Astra refused even under OpenAI's Daybreak Blue security program (1:32–3:02)
  • Anticlimax: Underneath is a standard anti-bot script traced to ServicePipe, a Russian anti-bot company (3:02–3:25)
Both sides use it
  • Attackers: Check Point found malware with a "this file is safe" injection in June 2025 (it didn't work), and Socket found a malicious Python package whose credential stealer began with a comment meant to trigger AI scanners' refusals (3:02–3:47)
  • Defenders: Tracebit's "context bombs" hidden in canary secrets stop attacking AI agents; across 152 runs, Claude Opus 4.8 reached admin in 93% of normal runs and 0% with one bomb, and across five models admin access dropped from 57% to 5% (3:47–5:18)
  • The trade-off: When Hugging Face investigated an AI-agent attack (17,000+ logged actions), a big lab's filters blocked them, so they used self-hosted GLM 5.2 instead (5:18–6:34)

This Gateway Makes Claude Code 30% Cheaper (Context Mode)

Better Stack · Andrus · uploaded 1 Nov, 7:30 AM IST · 7:51
Verdict: skim from 2:18 — useful cost numbers, but check the privacy trade-off.
🔁 Repeat: 'token costs / AI spend' — seen 16 times before, and 'context engineering' 22 times. New here: Context Mode's hosted gateway (Better Stack covered the open-source plugin in March) with side-by-side cost tests.
🔁 Repeat: 'AI gateway' — seen 7 times before. New here: a gateway that compresses tool output in the request path rather than routing models.
How it works
  • Base-URL proxy: It changes Claude Code's base URL so every request passes through Context Mode's servers (Anthropic still bills you); it folds big command output down to what matters, such as the one failing test out of 600, and archives the full version; setup adds three lines to settings (0:45–2:18)
Tests
  • NASA logs: On 3.4 million lines of 1995 Kennedy Space Center web logs, both agents correctly found the 9 AM 13 July peak (Discovery's STS-70 launch) and a 38-hour outage from Hurricane Erin; direct cost $0.68 vs $0.47 through the gateway, 31% less (2:18–3:50)
  • Asteroid data: Both found Apophis passing about 38,000 km from Earth on 13 April 2029; $0.84 vs $0.58, 32% less, overall $1.52 vs $1.05 from single runs (3:50–4:37)
  • Where the savings come from: The console claims about 12%; the rest seems to come from the gateway agent writing 27–37% less text, possibly from added instructions (unproven) (3:50–5:23)
Protect and caveats
  • Command firewall: The balanced preset blocked a push to main and reading .env, allowed a push to a branch, and had one false positive on a read-only command; it checks commands, not what scripts do, so it isn't a sandbox (4:37–6:10)
  • Privacy and pricing: Every prompt and tool output goes through their servers and isn't auto-deleted yet; free tier is 1,000 requests a month, Pro from $20; best for long log-heavy sessions, not quick fixes (6:10–7:46)
