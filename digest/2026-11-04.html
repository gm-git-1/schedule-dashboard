Why AI Makes You 100x Faster but Not Your Company - Mitchell Troyanovsky, Basis

AI Engineer · Mitchell Troyanovsky, Basis (co-founder) · uploaded 4 Nov, 12:00 AM IST · 19:13
Verdict: worth watching — a clear, practical framework for production agents, with behavior specs, forking and simulations as takeaways you can use.
🔁 Repeat: 'evals for agents' — seen 53 times before, 'AI for finance' 20 times, 'self-improving agents' 19 times and 'agent reliability' 4 times. New here: the "competency gap vs process gap" explanation of why personal AI speed-ups don't reach companies, behavior specs used as rubrics on trajectories with no ground truth, and cheap sub-agent "simulations" of your production agent.
Why companies haven't sped up
  • Firms run on process, not determinism: The head of an accounting firm trusts work he never sees because people follow processes, down to "use the approved software to pull the bank data"; Troyanovsky says his own output is up maybe 100x but a company's isn't (0:01–4:04)
  • Two gaps in coding-style agents: A competency gap (users must manage skills, memories and a theory of mind of the agent, which most knowledge workers can't) and a process gap (each user customises how work is done, when process should come from leadership) (3:03–6:04)
  • Be opinionated: Reading a PDF has endless options (VLM, open-source OCR, OCR plus 5 or 100 VLM checkers); an agent with no fixed opinion can't be reliable. Some opinions come from expertise, like reading the primary source for tax work or screenshotting a PowerPoint before returning it, and they trade off accuracy against latency (6:04–9:07)
Getting signal beyond outcome evals
  • Outcome evals don't generalize: A few hundred or thousand graded cases can't cover hundreds of thousands of long production trajectories, so instead check whether the intended behavior shows up, e.g. a bank reconciliation that explains a difference rather than posting a plug entry to make it tie (9:07–11:07)
  • Context is runtime training data: Behavior comes from the system prompt, progressive disclosure, tool descriptions and the model together, so care about every token, and write a "behavior spec" (a product spec for agents) of the opinions that matter most, used as a rubric on any trajectory: was the behavior relevant, and did the agent show it? (11:07–13:24)
  • Forking: When a trajectory fails a behavior check, fork it just before the bad decision to ask the agent why or test a context change, which beats guessing among a thousand inference calls in a 5-hour run (13:24–15:13)
  • Simulations: Ask a coding agent to spin up a sub-agent that pretends to be your production agent (same prompt and skills, told not to cheat) to replay a situation and test tweaks; he did this for a year and a half before Basis built forking (15:13–17:13)
Trust and scale
  • Earn trust like a coworker: You review a PR based on its blast radius and how competent the author is, and agents earn looser review the same way; nobody wants a "move 37" on their tax return, they want it done reliably (17:13–19:12)

Without Caching, Your AI Agent Costs 10x More - George Cameron, Artificial Analysis

AI Engineer · George Cameron, Artificial Analysis (co-founder) · uploaded 3 Nov, 8:00 PM IST · 19:43
Verdict: skim from 6:53 — the cost breakdown and the limited vs unlimited framing are new and useful; the landscape opening is familiar benchmark ground.
🔁 Repeat: 'open source / local models' — seen 50 times before, 'LLM releases / benchmarks' 46 times, 'inference / serving' 37 times and 'token costs / AI spend' 18 times. New here: a step-by-step cost breakdown of a single agent task, showing caching and turn count matter more than output tokens, plus the "limited vs unlimited intelligence" way to pick a model.
The landscape
  • Intelligence Index: Artificial Analysis (50 people in San Francisco, 500+ models tracked) blends 10 evals covering knowledge work, scientific reasoning, coding and terminal use, and hallucination; Opus 5.5 currently leads, and Gemini models stand out at navigating complex spreadsheets (2:47–5:25)
  • Pace and open weights (repeat): Frontier progress is accelerating, and open-weights models stay 3 to 9 months behind the leading closed models (5:25–6:53)
What an agent task actually costs
  • Two orders of magnitude apart: Opus and Fable cost over $5 per task while models like GPT-6 Luna cost about 7 cents and still beat the original GPT-4; the price of reaching any given intelligence level drops about 10x a year (for example from just over $1 to about 10 cents) (6:53–10:18)
  • Breaking down a $21 task: On their knowledge-work agent benchmark, Opus averages $21 per task: 165 steps at about 13 cents a call, with answer and reasoning tokens a tiny share; the big drivers are input tokens, cache-hit rate (cached input is about 10x cheaper) and number of turns (10:18–12:59)
  • Caching is the whole game: With a 0% cache-hit rate, for example a self-hosted setup without prompt caching, the same task costs over $200; they've started publishing cache-hit benchmarks for inference providers (12:59–13:38)
  • Reasoning effort compounds: Higher effort adds turns and bloats message history, so Opus 5.5 ranges from about $1 per task at low effort to $21 at max; the open-weights GLM 5.3 Flash is far cheaper on every driver (13:38–15:30)
Choosing a model
  • Limited vs unlimited intelligence tasks: "Analyse my cost drivers" always benefits from a smarter model, so set a budget; "how much did I spend on subscriptions?" gets the same $80 answer from cheap Luna and expensive Astra, so buy the cheapest model that clears the bar (15:30–18:12)
  • Shortlist, then benchmark your own: Read the Pareto curve, shortlist about five models and build your own benchmark for production; their new Optima product generates a benchmark from your uploaded context (18:12–19:42)

Make Your AI Agents Predictable With Graph Engineering - Zachary Blumenfeld, Neo4j

AI Engineer · Zachary Blumenfeld, Neo4j · uploaded 3 Nov, 10:00 AM IST · 20:36
Verdict: skim from 10:47 — the demo and timings are the new part; the memory-to-skill idea was covered in the earlier Neo4j talk.
🔁 Repeat: 'agent memory' — seen 17 times before and 'agent skills' 16 times, including Will Lyon's 8 Oct Neo4j talk on turning agent memory into skills, which this partly retells. Also 'decision models' 7 times and 'knowledge graphs' 5 times. New here: the "graph engineering" framing and a loan-approval demo with measured speed-ups from a compiled, graph-shaped skill.
What graph engineering means
  • Three kinds of graph: Graph engineering (a term popularised by a Peter Steinberger tweet this summer) is designing agent work as an explicit graph; Blumenfeld splits it into execution graphs (LangGraph, ADK-style workflows), context graphs of past decision traces, and procedural graphs where a skill's steps are nodes (0:01–3:04)
  • Why bother: Graphs give predictability (a constrained path), governance and auditing, and portability: stored next to your data in a knowledge layer, they can move between runtimes instead of living inside one framework (3:04–6:03)
Skills compiled into graphs
  • Research result: A Neo4j workshop paper compiled skill files, scripts and references into a graph the agent must follow, and it raised task reward across physics, computer science and security tasks using smaller models such as Sonnet and now Haiku (6:03–8:08)
  • Skills from memory (repeat): The same idea applied to the context graph distils a skill from past decision traces while keeping provenance, versioning and drift checks, in their reference Neo4j Agent Memory Service (8:08–9:22)
  • Customer uses: Consultancies automate compliance audit reports for asset managers by resolving tickers and entities across databases, and Uber keeps region-by-region driver and vehicle regulation logic in configuration graphs (9:22–10:47)
Loan-approval demo
  • Unconstrained agent: Asked whether to approve a loan for "Rosa's Bakery", the agent crawls the business-plan PDF, Databricks transactions, lending policy and past decisions, but two runs take different-length paths and its approve or escalate confidence isn't calibrated (10:47–14:30)
  • Compiled graph-shaped skill: Successful runs are compiled into a YAML graph skill run by a small CLI: hard policy rules in scripts, a typed decision model for the risk tier, a human-review router and a report-linting loop; runs are stored in the graph so you can see which branches were never taken (14:30–19:15)
  • Measured gains: Fable alone was right almost every time but took 2.7 minutes; the compiled procedure roughly halved that, and switching to Haiku cut it and the cost further, about half a minute to 2 minutes wall clock versus about 7.5 minutes before (19:15–20:15)

Your AI Agents Shouldn't Care Where They Run - Nikhil Harithas, Factory

AI Engineer · Nikhil Harithas, Factory (head of forward-deployed engineering) · uploaded 3 Nov, 9:00 AM IST · 17:13
Verdict: skim from 4:26 — worth it if you deploy agents into enterprise clouds; otherwise the sandbox and identity points are familiar.
🔁 Repeat: 'AI in regulated industries' — seen 22 times before, 'sandboxes for agents' 20 times, 'software factory' 16 times, 'agent identity' 9 times and 'forward-deployed engineering' 4 times. New here: the actual design of Factory's soon-to-be-open-sourced Open Compute layer (gateway, relay, drivers) for running agents inside a customer's own cloud.
The Blackstone problem
  • None of the defaults worked: Blackstone, with about a trillion dollars in assets, couldn't run agents on laptops and wanted its own compute, inference and identity, single-tenant everywhere, for Jira or Slack tickets delegated to Factory's coding agent Droid (0:12–2:44)
  • Agents can't keep borrowing your identity: The recurring questions were whom an agent acts for and where it runs; Harithas argues agents are just slightly non-deterministic applications and should use the same identity and deployment patterns as other software (2:14–4:26)
How Open Compute works
  • A separate compute plane: Installing an agent on a machine wasn't enough, because customers still had to manage the compute lifecycle, so Factory built Open Compute to handle it for any agent, on EC2, Kubernetes, micro-VMs or sandbox providers such as Daytona or Modal (4:26–6:17)
  • Gateway, drivers and relay: A session request goes to Factory's compute gateway, which asks the Open Compute controller in the customer's VPC to start a machine through a driver (one per compute type); the machine then opens an outbound websocket to Factory's relay, so no inbound connection is ever needed, which security teams prefer (6:17–9:19)
  • Bring your own key: Model calls can go through the customer's own deal, for example Bedrock, so the non-deterministic part of the loop and its blast radius stay inside the VPC (9:19–11:21)
Open source and the hard parts
  • Computers, not sessions: Open-sourcing is planned "in a few weeks"; the unit is a computer with pause, resume, snapshot and fork (useful for handing subtasks to sub-agents), and snapshots and secrets can live in the customer's own S3 and secret store (11:21–14:22)
  • Lifecycle bookkeeping: Dropped acks, routing users to the right sandbox and AWS micro-VMs' 8-hour cap (their multi-day "missions" must snapshot and rehydrate in place) are the hard parts; new drivers are easy because Droid can write one straight from the spec (14:22–17:13)

Better Search Took This AI Agent From 36% to 98% Accuracy - Aamir Shakir, Mixedbread

AI Engineer · Aamir Shakir, Mixedbread · uploaded 3 Nov, 8:00 AM IST · 16:30
Verdict: skim from 9:04 — a vendor pitch whose new parts are the multi-vector model and the hedge-fund numbers; the RAG critique is well-worn.
🔁 Repeat: 'AI for finance' — seen 20 times before, 'RAG vs long context' 12 times, 'multimodal models' 8 times and 'agentic search' 7 times. New here: Mixedbread's multi-vector (late-interaction) retrieval model and a hedge-fund case going from 36% to 98% answer accuracy; the "RAG is lossy" first half is familiar.
Why agents fail to find things
  • Grep doesn't scale: An analyst asking why Intel's new GPU uses LPDDR5 instead of HBM memory gets so many lexical hits that the agent reads every document, which Shakir says can push a basic query to $10–$20, and scanned or binary PDFs can't be grepped at all (1:30–4:00)
  • The "Frankenstein stack" (repeat): OCR or transcription, chunking, a single-vector embedding, a vector database and a reranker each lose information, need a vendor each, and take 6–8 months and 4–5 people to stand up (4:00–6:02)
  • One point per page fails at scale (repeat): Nvidia's 1996 and 2026 earnings filings embed to nearly the same point, so quality is fine at tens of documents but degrades in production, and agents compensate by spawning sub-agents and burning context (6:02–9:04)
What Mixedbread built
  • Multi-vector retrieval: Retrieval barely moved from BM25 (1994) until 2022 embeddings, then improved slowly; their new model, out "next week", creates many contextualised embeddings per page (net income, revenue, all tagged as Nvidia) from screenshots, while still returning OCR and layout (9:04–11:08)
  • Demo on Intel reports: A query for Intel Foundry's Q2 2025 operating margin finds the right chart page with bounding boxes for citations, normally in under 20 ms; a qualitative question triggers their agentic-search model "Toast 1", which they claim matches Astra and Fable while being 40x faster and cheaper (11:08–14:57)
Result
  • 36% to 98%: A large hedge fund with over a billion documents, onboarded in about a week and a half, went from 36% to 98% expert-graded answer accuracy after replacing its chunking and vector-database stack, and used about 3x fewer LLM tokens (14:57–16:29)
